3Nine rules, and what each one rejects

Forty screens are too many to hold in the head. These nine rules generate them. If a state is missing from this document, apply the rules and you will get the same answer I would have drawn. Each one names the option it rejected, so the engineer knows an omission was a decision.

3.1 Below 420 px the catalogue is one column, not two

The titles are not too long. The column is too narrow. At 328 px every title the site owns fits in three lines with nothing clamped, and 77 of the 86 fit in two.

Rejected — keep two columns and ask editorial for shorter headlines. That moves a layout failure onto someone else's desk, and it would have to be re-litigated on every article ever written. It also cannot work: the headlines are load-bearing SEO on a site whose demand is named-entity lookups, and the four longest are the Real Wedding titles that name the venue.

Rejected — keep two columns and drop to 15 px. Still cuts 36%, and 15 px Georgia at 156 px is a worse read than 17 px at 328.

3.2 A dense list is a list row with a thumbnail, never a smaller card

One column of 4:3 cards is honest but enormous: twelve of them is roughly 4,000 px before the footer. The list row — an 80 px square thumbnail, the title at 15 px beside it, the category beneath — is about 96 px tall, so the same twelve occupy about 1,150 px and still truncate nothing. So: the first item of a set is a card, the rest are rows.

Rejected — twelve full-width cards. It buries the footer and the category rail four screens down for no gain in comprehension.

3.3 A title is never clamped below the measured maximum for its container

Clamping is a last resort, not a layout tool. Where a clamp must exist it is set at the measured worst case for that box, and the full title stays the element's accessible name. In the containers specified here that means no clamp is needed anywhere; the only clamp in this document is on the hero deck, at four lines, where the longest real deck is 145 characters.

Rejected — line-clamp: 3 everywhere, as shipped. It throws away 46 of 95 characters on the worst case and cuts two-thirds of the corpus.

3.4 Three different kinds of nothing get three different sentences

Production has one sentence for all of them, and using it in the second case makes it false. The three are not the same event and the way out of each is different:

What happenedWhat the reader needsScreen
We have not written it. A planned section with no articles in it.To know the section is real but bare, and to be handed the articles that are here.K4

Rejected — “akan datang tidak lama lagi” as the universal empty copy. It is a promise the company cannot date. It has been on /artikel/nikah-undang-undang since Sprint 02 and the articles have not arrived. DES-06's retrospective caught exactly this shape once already — an offline message promising cached reading the build could not do. A page should say what is true now and offer what exists now.

3.5 A request that failed is never drawn as a result that was empty

DES-06 read this defect out of the deployed search bundle: the catch branch sets results to [], so a dropped connection renders the same screen as “we have nothing on your topic”. The rule generalises to every surface here. An empty state says there is nothing. An error state says we could not fetch it and offers the same action again. They never share a drawing.

Consequence for the build, and it is a hard dependency: the API must return a status distinguishable from an empty array. Without it, K6, A4 and DES-06's F1 cannot be built and the defect that costs the most stays shipped.

3.6 Nothing a reader must see is client-only

Measured with scripting disabled: the homepage, the catalogue and the article all render in full — 1,343, 1,584 and 10,318 characters of <main> respectively. The 404 renders nothing: no <main>, zero characters, a blank page. The runtime error boundary is client-only by construction, so a failure during server render produces no boundary at all.

The rule: not-found, empty and error screens are server-rendered like every other page. They are the screens a reader hits when things are already going wrong, which is exactly when the JavaScript is least likely to have arrived.

Rejected — treat the blank 404 as acceptable because most readers have JavaScript. The reader who lands on a dead link on a cold route is the reader on the worst connection in the sample. On the routes measured at 21.5–25.9 s, that reader is looking at a blank white page for that long. Measured directly: /cari?q=mas+kahwin returned its empty 404 body after 23.79 seconds. Every crawler that does not execute scripts sees an empty page too.

3.7 A tap is acknowledged in under 100 ms, on the thing that was tapped

Not with a full-page skeleton. The reader is standing on a page they can still read; replacing it with grey boxes takes that away and lies about the layout of a page that has not arrived. Instead, three layers that escalate with time:

  1. Immediately. The tapped link takes its pressed state and keeps it.
  2. At 300 ms. A 2 px indeterminate bar appears under the header. The page beneath stays live and readable.
  3. At 3 s. The bar is joined by one line of text under the header: Sedang dimuatkan… and, on a route this slow, that line is the only honest thing on screen. It is in an aria-live="polite" region so a screen reader hears it once.

Skeletons are used in exactly one place — the first paint of a route that has no previous page to stand on, i.e. a cold entry from search. There the skeleton mirrors the real block sizes, which are known because the layout is fixed.

Rejected — a skeleton on every navigation. It discards a readable page and, on a 23-second route, shows a fake page for 23 seconds.

3.8 Furniture never outweighs the article

Measured: marriott-putrajaya gives a 6,610 px page to 578 px of writing. The rule: below 1,500 characters of body, the article drops its table of contents, its share bar and its gallery button, and the related block moves up to take their place. A short entry should read as a short entry, not as a long page that failed to load.

Rejected — hide short articles, or pad them. Both are dishonest, and eight of the ten shortest are venue entries that SEO-04 is actively expanding.

3.9 The table of contents fires on length, not on heading count

Measured rule in production: 3 or more h2. Measured consequence: twelve articles over 4,000 characters have no contents list, including the longest article on the site at 25,955 characters and every mas-kahwin-* page, because those are written entirely in h3. Those are the pages the site's clicks arrive on.

New rule: the contents list appears at 3,000 body characters or more, and it lists every h2 and h3 in document order. Above 15 entries it collapses to the current section plus a control that opens the rest — a 25-item list is 845 px, 70% of a 360 px viewport, and each row in it is 17 px tall against a 44 px floor.

Rejected — leave the heading-count rule and ask writers to add h2s. It re-writes 12 published articles to fix a display rule, and the next writer who uses h3 correctly breaks it again.

10.2 Focus order, and the two places it is moved deliberately

Focus follows document order everywhere except two: a retry control after an error, and the results heading after a filter. Both are cases where the reader acted and the consequence is somewhere they are not looking. Focus is never moved on a page load that succeeded, and never trapped.

  • A skip link to #kandungan is the first focusable element on every page. The category rail is 9 to 15 links; a keyboard reader must be able to pass it.
  • The rail is one tab stop, then arrow keys within it, so the rail costs one stop rather than fifteen.
  • The chip block opened from Semua 15 takes focus on its first chip and returns focus to the control when closed.
  • The read-next bar is in the tab order at the foot of the article, where it appears in the document, not floated to the front.
