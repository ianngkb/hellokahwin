## Every hukum / adat separation made

Each article carries an explicit section. This is the pillar's differentiator and
it is not buried.

**A1 — `Yang mana tuntutan agama, yang mana adat`**

- Religion: making doa for the couple (Mufti WP: dianjurkan dalam Islam); the
  Abu Daud 2130 wording as doa ma'thur; attending a personal walimah invitation
  (wajib per jumhur, sunat per Hanafi, attributed to Jabatan Mufti Negeri Selangor).
- Adat: written greeting cards; pantun on the card and at the majlis; money in an
  envelope and its amount; wishes like "semoga cepat dapat cahaya mata", flagged
  as a good wish that is not a narrated lafaz.
- Also separated inside the invitation section: a **general** invitation
  (WhatsApp group, Facebook event) does not create the obligation; a **personal**
  one does. This is the single most-misunderstood point on the topic and it comes
  straight from Irsyad al-Fatwa 296.

**A2 — `Yang mana tuntutan agama, yang mana adat`**, as a four-row table

- Sunat: making doa after the akad; placing a hand on the wife's head while
  reciting.
- Digalakkan on the practice of the sahabat: the couple's two-rakaat prayer.
- **Adat semata-mata: the batal air sembahyang ceremony.** Mufti WP says this in
  as many words. Stated with the note that it is not forbidden, only that a family
  who skips it has left no religious obligation undone.
- Also separated: the Yusuf/Zulaikha/Musa/Safurah/Sulaiman/Balqis doa is reported
  as an adab question with Mufti WP's actual answer, which does not prohibit it.
  We report; we do not rule.

**A3 — `Yang mana tuntutan agama, yang mana urusan majlis`**

- Religion: making doa after the akad (sunat, al-Nawawi via Mufti WP); the adab
  of not deliberately rhyming a doa.
- Administration, not religion: the three-part structure, the 2–3 minute limit
  and the pembaca criteria are all **JAKIM administrative guidance for government
  ceremonies, not a fatwa**. Stated twice, because the criterion "pembaca doa
  hendaklah seorang lelaki" reads as a religious ruling if left unqualified and
  it is not one.
- Also administration: a printed aturcara, a stage and microphone, engaging a
  named ustaz, projecting the text on a screen.
- **Scope stated honestly:** JAKIM's guideline covers majlis rasmi and separuh
  rasmi kerajaan. A kenduri at home is neither, and the 2026 edition no longer
  carries a wedding doa at all. What survives is its closing statement that it
  hopes to help all layers of society when they pray. The article says exactly
  that rather than implying the rules bind a kenduri, and it adds the practical
  consequence: reading a doa in Malay at a kenduri breaks nothing, because
  6.2 (vi) binds government majlis texts and not a father speaking in his own
  living room.

## One lesson worth the board's time

Two different defects were found in this run, and only one of them was findable
by the method we were using.

**Findable by the edition audit:** every stale figure and stale clause. Both
writers went at the JAKIM PDFs hard on the question "which edition is current",
and that question found the 5-minute duration, the *seelok-eloknya* wording, the
dropped wedding doa, and the 6.2 (vi) language reversal.

**Not findable by it:** A1's language FAQ was citing a clause about *spoken doa
at an official majlis* to justify the language of a *written greeting card*. That
is a category error, not an edition error. The clause was quoted accurately, from
the correct edition, with the correct paragraph number. Re-checking the edition a
third time would never have surfaced it, because nothing about the citation was
stale. It surfaced only because someone asked a different question: not "is this
current" but "is this clause even about the thing this sentence is about".

The P7 writer named the same shape in their own C7.4 one level quieter, as an
omission rather than a statement: a checklist naming the language for two items
and staying silent on the others, leaving the reader to infer the opposite of
what the source says. An inference has no wrong sentence in it for a reviewer to
find.

**A third variant appeared after this section was first written**, and it sharpens
the point rather than softening it. The 6.1 flattening was not a wrong citation,
a stale citation, or a clause applied to the wrong object. It was an accurate
citation **summarised more tightly than the source reads**, and A3 contradicted
its own table because of it. The edition audit could not see it, and neither could
the category audit I ran after the A1 catch, because I was checking whether each
clause governed its object rather than whether my summary preserved the clause's
scope.

**What this suggests for the Editorial Review Board.** Verification currently asks
whether a claim is true and current. That is necessary and it worked. Three
further questions each caught something the previous ones could not:

1. Does the cited rule govern the situation this sentence applies it to? (A1)
2. Does the summary preserve every allowance and exception the source states? (A3)
3. Does any list or checklist imply a rule by naming it for some items and staying
   silent on others? (A3, and the P7 writer's C7.4)

Every defect of these three kinds in this run was caught by another person reading
with a different question in mind, never by re-running the check that found the
previous one. That is the argument for keeping a second reader on procedural and
religious content, rather than for adding another pass of the same check.

**A fourth defect then landed on question 1 again**, and where it landed is the
part worth recording. The undated edition's mukaddimah clause was sitting in the
isi kandungan row of the three-edition comparison table — the very table built to
stop edition and clause confusion. Building an artifact that makes a class of
error visible does not exempt the artifact from that class of error, and nobody
re-audits the checking tool. Worth one line in whatever the board reads: the
comparison table is content, and it gets verified like content.

**In every case the detailed passage was accurate and the summarising sentence
overstated.** Never the reverse. Not once did a careful passage get the rule wrong
while a summary got it right.

That is not coincidence, and the mechanism is visible. We write the detailed
passage with the source open, so it carries the source's hedges, exceptions and
allowances. We write the summary afterwards from what we now believe, and a
summary earns its place by being shorter, so the first things cut are exactly the
qualifiers the source put there on purpose. The style guide already tells us to
answer in the first sixty words and to prefer the shorter word, and both of those
pressures push in this direction.

**Suggested rule, for the Managing Editor to word:** where an article summarises a
rule it states in full elsewhere on the page, the summary must carry every
exception the full statement carries, or it must not state the rule at all. And
the review check that finds this is not "is the citation correct" but "does the
summary agree with the passage it summarises". On this run that check would have
caught all three, and nothing else did.
