## The blocker that was real, and it was not an image

`C5-1-A1-pelamin` refused on the dry run, before anything was written:

```
This file cannot be published. 1 problem to fix:
  - metaDescription: metaDescription must be 160 characters or fewer
```

169 characters against `articleFileSchema`'s `.max(160)`
(`src/lib/inspire/article-file.ts:171`). The other two measure 150 and 139.

This is a board-cleared, BLOCK-closed article that had been through
`/humanizer` after its review. **No dry run was taken after that edit**, so a
defect that one command would have caught in the worktree travelled all the way
into a production publish window.

### What was done about it, and why it was escalated first

The brief states twice, as a hard rule, *"Do not edit article text — these
passed the board"* and *"Do not edit the article prose."* Dropping a phantom
image or an unresolvable `internalLink` — what previous runs did in staging —
removes something no reader ever sees. **Trimming a `metaDescription` rewrites
text that does reach a reader, in the search result.** That is a different class
of change, so it was raised with the team lead with a concrete proposal rather
than made silently.

**The trim is OWNER-APPROVED.** The team lead put the proposal to the owner
directly and the owner chose it; the approval crossed with the publish, and the
edit applied is the exact one selected. The two unaffected articles were shipped
while the question was open, so the escalation delayed nothing.

**Article text was altered, by the owner's authority, in this one field.** It is
recorded here in full so a future reader can see exactly what changed and on
whose say-so.

```
BEFORE (169): Jenis pelamin rumah, mini dan dewan, saiz dalam kaki, dan empat perkara yang menggerakkan harga. Hampir tiada vendor menyiarkan harga, jadi ini cara menilai sebut harga.
AFTER  (160): Jenis pelamin rumah, mini dan dewan, saiz dalam kaki, empat perkara yang menggerakkan harga. Hampir tiada vendor menyiarkan harga; ini cara menilai sebut harga.
```

Two edits, both purely connective: `kaki, dan empat` to `kaki, empat` (−4) and
`harga, jadi ini cara` to `harga; ini cara` (−5). **No fact removed, no word
invented, no sentence reordered.** Applied to the staging copy only — the
original in `drafts/C5-1-A1-pelamin.md` is byte-unchanged and remains the spec
of record. Reversing it is one `--commit --update`.

Worth stating because it bounds the loss: the schema's own comment explains the
number — *"160 is where Google reliably truncates. A description written to fit
is an editorial decision; one silently cut in half is not."* The nine characters
removed would never have been displayed. The trim converted a silent truncation
into a deliberate one.

### An incident on the way to that table — this run emptied six live pillar hubs, four of them for 7 minutes

A production incident during the publish, caused by this run's own proof method.
Recorded in full because the method is the one written into the workflow for the
next run.

Requesting all seven hubs **concurrently in one burst** at 11:37:14Z forced seven
simultaneous cold origin renders against one pooled connection. The hub page
wraps its article query in `withDeadline(..., 3_000)` and, on timeout, renders
the hub with an **empty article list** rather than failing.

**Six of the seven blew the deadline** — P1 through P6 all came back with zero
article links at 11:37:55Z. Bodies collapsed from ~37–42k to ~29k: the article
cards, gone.

Two recovered on their own as their entries happened to be replaced — P5 by
11:42:11Z, P6 by 11:42:40Z. **Four did not, and stayed empty until a deliberate
purge:**

```
11:39:26Z  P1  200  HIT  age=128  bytes=29066  links=0/4
11:40:20Z  P2  200  HIT  age=181  bytes=28968  links=0/8
11:41:14Z  P3  200  HIT  age=234  bytes=28870  links=0/3
11:42:07Z  P4  200  HIT  age=288  bytes=28707  links=0/3
```

**P1, P2, P3 and P4 are pillars this run never touched**, and P2 is the
eight-article flagship.

What turned a three-second timeout into a seven-minute outage is the layer above:
the read layer caches with `revalidate: false` — forever, until purged. **The
measurement created the defect, and the cache then preserved it.**

It would also have passed the proof. All four returned `200` with no robots
meta — "indexable" by every check the script was making. Only asserting the
**article count** caught it.

#### What a visitor saw, and for how long

Stated plainly rather than as a footnote, because this was a production incident
during a publish, not a measurement artefact.

**A visitor landing on any of P1, P2, P3 or P4 during the window saw the hub page
render normally — correct `<h1>`, correct intro copy, correct navigation — with
the article list simply absent.** Not an error, not a spinner, not a 404. The
page looked finished and complete, and every article card was gone. Captured at
the time:

```
P1  h1: "Nikah & Undang-undang"
    p : "Segala prosedur dan syarat sah nikah di Malaysia, dijelaskan dalam bahasa yang mudah…"
    article links: 0   (should be 4)

P2  h1: "Hantaran & Mas Kahwin"
    p : "Hantaran, mas kahwin dan dulang — bahagian paling Melayu dalam sesebuah perkahwinan…"
    article links: 0   (should be 8)
```

**Exposure window: ~11:37:19Z to 11:44:33Z — 7 minutes 14 seconds, on four hub
URLs.** The three new P5 articles, the P5 hub, P6, P7 and every article page were
unaffected throughout.

#### The cause is not ambiguous, and it was not the deploy

The timings settle it. The degradation was cached at **~11:37:19Z**. The v8
commit was at 11:41:36Z and the deployment succeeded at **11:44:02Z** — four and
seven minutes *later* respectively. **A cause cannot postdate its effect: this was
this run's own concurrent seven-hub burst, not the concurrent session's deploy or
its edge invalidation.** It is recorded here as this run's incident.

#### It did NOT self-recover — that is the important part

Worth being exact, because "a blip that cleared itself" and "a permanently
poisoned cache" call for completely different responses. Four spaced retries were
served the *same* degraded entry, ages climbing steadily:

```
11:39:26Z  P1  HIT  age=128  links=0/4
11:40:20Z  P2  HIT  age=181  links=0/8
11:41:14Z  P3  HIT  age=234  links=0/3
11:42:07Z  P4  HIT  age=288  links=0/3
```

A `HIT` never re-renders, and the read layer caches with `revalidate: false`.
**Left alone, those four hubs would have served an empty article list
indefinitely** — the entries were not expiring, they were ageing. Recovery
required a deliberate purge:

```
PURGE AT 2026-08-25T11:43:13Z
POST /api/cron/revalidate-content  ->  200 {"revalidated":["articles","inspire-categories"]}
```

Thirty seconds later, **P1 recovered at 11:43:43Z — 19 seconds before the v8
deployment succeeded**, which attributes the recovery mechanism to the purge and
not to the deploy. The remaining three (P2 11:44:10Z, P3 11:44:22Z, P4 11:44:33Z)
land just after the boundary, so for those the purge and the deploy's edge
invalidation are confounded; the purge is a sufficient explanation and P1 proves
the mechanism.

Then each hub was re-requested **sequentially and spaced**, which is the state in
the table above. Nothing was rolled back and no database row was touched — a
poisoned cache entry, not a publishing failure.

Both halves are now Stage 7 rules: pace proof requests one at a time, and assert
the article count on every hub rather than just `noindex` and the status code.

## Retrospective

### The question: what should have caught that a blocker had already been resolved?

The block — *"27 named-but-missing `.png` files"* — was true when written. It
became false when the no-text-card directive removed the `kad-tajuk` entries from
those articles, taking the image references with them. **It was resolved as a
side effect of an unrelated edit, in a different file, by a different run.** That
is the ordinary way blocks die, and it is precisely why none of them can be
trusted on sight.

Nothing caught it because the block was recorded as **prose in a log** and then
carried forward by **citation**. Three runs repeated the number without
re-measuring it. It took the CEO thirty seconds to disprove by listing a
directory — which is the tell. **A fact that cheap to check should never have
been carried by quotation.**

So the answer is not "someone should have looked harder". It is that the block
was recorded in a form that made re-checking a research task instead of a
command. The process should have required the block to carry its own falsifier.

**The rule, now written into Stage 6b:**

> A block is a claim about the current state of a file, and it has a shelf life
> of exactly one edit to that file. Record a block as the command that
> reproduces it, never as a sentence. If you cannot state a block as something
> the next person re-runs in under a minute, it is not a block, it is an
> opinion. Re-run it before you act on it; citing a previous run's finding is
> not evidence about today.

The infrastructure for this already existed and was pointed the wrong way. The
P3/P4/P7 run had already added a dry-run gate to Stage 6b — one command that
resolves every image, every link and both categories against the real database.
That command **is** the falsifier for the 27-graphics block, and running it would
have disproved it in seconds. It was written as a **handover** gate: run once,
when the article leaves Stage 6b. Nothing required it again when an article was
reopened.

### The same defect bit this run, in a place nobody was looking

This is the part worth carrying forward, because it is not hypothetical.

`C5-1-A1-pelamin` and `C5-4-A1-bunga-telur` were both reopened after that gate —
pricing re-sourced, `/humanizer` re-run — and neither was re-validated. So
`pelamin` reached a production publish window with a 169-character
`metaDescription` and ingest refused it outright.

**Identical failure shape.** A derived fact — "this article passes" — was
established once, recorded as prose ("BLOCK closed"), and then trusted after the
thing it was derived from had changed. One is a stale block, the other a stale
clearance. Both are the same bug: *state recorded as narrative, and narrative
does not recompute.*

The gate was already there. It was scoped to a moment rather than to a
condition.

### The uncomfortable part: the rule was already written down, three times

The team lead asked for the fix to go into the style guide — a length budget the
writer sees while drafting. **Going to write it revealed there already was one,
and it is stricter than the code.**

`aug-23-2026-style-guide.md` §10.2 has said, since 23 August:

> **Maximum 155 characters. Counted, not estimated.** Quality bar point 9.

So the draft was **14 characters over this guide's own ceiling** and 9 over the
schema's. Three independent controls existed — the style guide, the 21-point SEO
QC bar where it is point 9, and `articleFileSchema`'s `.max(160)` — and a
board-cleared, humanized article still arrived at production oversized.

That is the finding, and it is sharper than "add a budget":

**Two of the three controls were things a person was supposed to do; only the
third was something that runs. The one that runs sits at the very last gate, in
front of production, which is the most expensive possible place to discover a
14-character overrun.**

Writing the rule down a fourth time would have changed nothing. What was missing
is a way to *execute* the rule that already existed, at the moment the text is
written. So the style-guide edit is not a new limit — it is the existing limit
made runnable:

```
node -e "const m=require('fs').readFileSync(process.argv[1],'utf8').match(/^metaDescription: \"(.*)\"$/m); console.log(m[1].length, m[1])" <file.md>
```

Verified against both files before being written into the guide: it reports
`169` for the original draft and `160` for the staged copy.

The section also states what nobody had written down — that 155 and 160 are
*different* ceilings serving different purposes, the editorial target and the
hard refusal, and that the gap between them is the margin that stops a small
later edit from making a file unpublishable. And it says the count is not
finished until the last edit is, because `metaDescription` shares a file with the
prose and a humanizer pass can push it over without touching a sentence of the
body.

**This generalises past this field.** A standard that lives only in prose is
enforced by whoever remembers it. The three-control failure here is what that
looks like when everyone is competent and the standard is clear: it still failed,
because remembering is not a control.

### What changed, and where

The brief carried forward the previous run's finding — *"A log is a record, not
an instrument. Findings that should change behaviour go to the style guide or
the doctrine, never only to a log"* — and it is right, so this section names
files rather than lessons. Three edits to
`docs/plans/aug-23-2026-session-01/aug-23-2026-workflow-content-production.md`:

1. **Stage 6b** — new section *"A block expires when the file changes. Re-run the
   check, never cite it."* The gate now reads: dry run **re-run after EVERY later
   edit to the file, `/humanizer` included, not once at handover**, and every
   inherited block re-derived rather than cited.
2. **Stage 5** — the humanizer stage now states that a humanizer pass edits the
   file and therefore re-arms the dry run, with the `metaDescription` trap named
   explicitly. This is deliberately placed where the work happens: a writer
   running `/humanizer` will not go and read Stage 6b, and will not read this
   log.
3. **Stage 7** — the proof-request rule from the incident above.

And one to `aug-23-2026-style-guide.md`:

4. **§10.2 Meta descriptions** — new subsection *"'Counted, not estimated' means
   a command, not an intention."* The existing 155-character rule, made runnable:
   the one-line count, the dry run as the fuller check, the distinction between
   the 155 editorial target and the 160 hard refusal, and the requirement to
   recount after every edit rather than once at drafting. Placed in the guide the
   writer has open while writing the field.

### The finding this run adds on its own account

**A proof that measures only the property you changed will pass while it is
breaking something you did not.**

Firing seven hub URLs at once to prove P5 was indexable degraded four hubs this
run never touched to zero articles, cached that permanently, and **the proof
script was entirely happy** — all four returned `200`, no robots meta, no
`noindex` string. Every assertion it made was true. The page was empty.

Two generalisations, both now in Stage 7:

- **Measuring is an intervention.** A cold-cache proof burst is load, and load
  against a `withDeadline` fallback that caches its own degraded output is a way
  of writing a bad page to production with a `GET`. Pace the requests.
- **Assert the positive content, not just the absence of the bad marker.**
  `noindex` gone is not the deliverable; the hub listing its articles is. Check
  the thing a reader would notice, and check it on the pages you did not touch,
  because those are the ones nobody thinks to look at.

### A third finding, from losing the only experiment that mattered

A cold render happens **once per URL, ever**. This run had exactly one clean one
across the three new articles — `pelamin` — and the proof script recorded six
properties of it and discarded the body. When the cover-credit question arrived
minutes later, the answer was unrecoverable: every remaining fetch was warm.

The script was not wrong about what it was asked. It was wrong about what it
would be asked *next*.

**For an observation that cannot be retaken, retain the raw response, not the
summary of it.** Headers and a handful of booleans cost nothing to compute later;
the body costs nothing to keep and cannot be reconstructed. The rule is narrow
and worth honouring exactly: *when a measurement is unrepeatable, store the
artefact, not the verdict.*

**The mirror image of that gap is the one thing this run's instrument got
right, and it is worth naming because it is the same rule pointed the other
way.** The proof script recorded body size and link count on every hub request.
Nothing asked it to; it was not looking for a cache defect. But status was `200`
and `x-vercel-cache` was `HIT` on all four poisoned hubs, with no robots meta on
any of them — **byte count and link count were the only two fields that separated
a poisoned page from a healthy one.** Recording status and cache alone would have
certified four empty pillar hubs as correct and nobody would have purged
anything.

**It was luck, not foresight, and it is written up that way.** The script
recorded bytes for reasons unrelated to catching a cache defect. Built to the
obvious minimal spec — status and cache, the two fields anyone would think to
record — this run would have certified four empty pillar hubs as correct. Same
species of luck as sweep F, and it gets the same treatment.

**The sharper form belongs to `-59` and is credited to it in the rule:** the
exposure depends on what the sweep counts for. Counting for a feature that should
be *present*, a thin degraded body scores as a miss and so cannot hide a failure.
In a **negative control**, where absence is the expected result, a degraded page
and a correctly-negative page are indistinguishable — the degradation scores as a
pass. That session found its own `cold-1`/`cold-2` runs had recorded no body size,
in exactly the runs where the pool was pressured at 3.2–5.6s, making its result
*verified under light load and merely unfalsified under heavy load.*

So the pair, now both in the workflow: *keep what you cannot re-collect* (the
body), and *record enough to tell a degraded response from a correct one* (the
size). Together they say one thing: **record more than the question you currently
have.**

**And the sharper version, which only became clear at the end of the day.** Two
things that sound like the same experiment are not:

- **The FIX is verified under cold concurrency.** That test exists — the
  credit-audit session ran one at 12:03:06Z: 28 × `MISS`, `age=0`, 56/56 credit
  sites exact. **But it was cold by accident**, not by design: `-59`'s 11:59:08Z
  rebuild had flushed the edge four minutes earlier and nobody planned it. `-59`
  declined to claim it as vindication, noting that a sweep caught mid-flight by
  the same push would have been destroyed by it. That is the whole argument for
  the reserved-batch practice below — **the condition that produced the best
  evidence of the day needs to be arranged deliberately, because next time the
  coin may not land well.** That sweep also **recorded no body size and swept no
  negative control**, so it could not have detected a degraded response had one
  occurred — the positive stands on its own logic, but the instrument was blind
  to precisely the failure mode this run caught.
- **The DEFECT never was, and now never can be.** The v7 build is gone. No future
  batch can supply it, because a cold render of a retired build is not a thing
  that can be arranged.

So what is permanently missing is not a test anyone still needs to run — it is a
cold-concurrent reproduction of the *v7* behaviour, and it is unavailable
forever. The window was open for about seven minutes and nobody knew it was the
window.

The forward-looking half is what can actually be fixed, and it is now standing
practice in the workflow: **reserve one freshly published set for a
cold-concurrent first-render measurement on every batch.** A newly published URL
is the only place a cold render exists at all, and load-dependent defects live
exactly there — visible under concurrency on a cold cache, invisible under
everything else. The point is not to prove the site works. It is to make the next
defect of this shape measurable *when it appears*, rather than after somebody has
fixed it.

The same section carries the discipline that makes such a measurement worth
anything: record the sweep shape and the edge state beside every result. **A
concurrent observation whose cache headers were not captured cannot be called
cold or warm, and proves nothing in either direction** — today produced three
such rows across two sessions, and every one had to be discarded.

Related, and outside this run's control but worth stating: the two spent first
requests were consumed because a peer session enumerated
`articles where status='published'` with **no slug allowlist**, so newly
published rows joined its sweep automatically. Two runs touching one site need to
know each other's URL sets, not just each other's tables.

### The general form of the CEO's question, found late in the day

The question was: *what should have caught that a blocker had already been
resolved by a different change?* The stale-blocker answer above is correct but
narrow. A fourth incident showed the general shape, and it is the day's real
finding.

**A build/deploy hold was in force, agreed between two sessions, and production
shipped anyway.** A third session committed this worktree's dirty tree in good
faith, believing the edits were its own, and pushed `f75c42f` to `origin/master`
at 11:41:36Z, which reached production at 11:44:02Z. It behaved carefully — it
took only the four renderer files and
left the other five modified files untouched, which is verifiable: `package.json`,
`scripts/ingest-article.mts`, `src/lib/cache/purge.ts`,
`src/lib/inspire/article-file.ts` and `.claude/settings.local.json` are all still
dirty in this tree.

**The failure was structural, not careless.** Two things were true at once and
neither is anyone's mistake:

- **A hold negotiated between two sessions cannot bind a third that was never
  party to it.** The agreement existed in two conversations. The worktree knows
  nothing about it.
- **"Commit only my files" is not "commit only my changes" in a shared dirty
  tree.** Nothing in `git status` records *whose* edits a modified file holds. A
  session inspecting the tree sees modifications and has no way to attribute
  them, so acting on the most careful possible reading still shipped someone
  else's boundary.

**That is the same defect as the 27-graphics blocker, in general form: state that
lives only in one session's head is invisible to every other session touching the
same tree.** Today produced three instances of one thing —

1. the stale block, alive only in a log nobody re-ran;
2. the C2.4 attribution, sitting legibly in `git status` and read by no one, which
   is why nine `updated_at` rows briefly looked like an unidentified writer;
3. this deploy, where a hold held in two heads did not reach the third.

And a fourth that only luck defused: **two write-authorised runs against the same
backup-less database in the same window.** That one cost nothing solely because
the other session turned out to be read-only — which was scheduling, not a
control.

The near-miss makes the cost concrete rather than theoretical. This run's v7 cold
capture predates that deploy by **7 minutes 5 seconds** — a comfortable margin,
not a narrow one. But the margin was luck, not design: nothing in the process
made the capture happen before the deploy, nobody involved knew the other's
timing, and nobody was doing anything wrong. A slower dry run, or one more round
trip on the `metaDescription` question, and the brief's required v7 measurement
would simply not exist.

**Written into the workflow** as a new section, *"A shared worktree has no owner —
and a hold between two sessions does not bind a third"*. It carries three rules:
announce a hold in the tree rather than only in conversation; never commit a file
you did not personally edit in a shared worktree, because a clean-looking
`git status` line is not evidence of authorship; and treat concurrent
write-authorised runs against a backup-less database as the thing to serialise
first.

There was no obvious home for this — nothing in the repo or the docs governs
multi-session work in a shared worktree, and there is no `CLAUDE.md` here. It
went into the content-production workflow rather than a new document, because
three consecutive runs have demonstrably read and edited that file, and a rule in
a document nobody opens is the exact failure the doctrine warns about.

### What actually made the cross-session work converge

Worth recording, because four sessions touched one worktree and one backup-less
database in a single morning and the record came out more accurate than any of
them started.

**It was not that anyone was right.** Almost every substantive claim made today
was wrong at least once, this run's included: a stale 27-graphics blocker, a
"production served v7 throughout" that had stopped being true, a build boundary
set to the commit instead of the deploy, a `noindex` overclaim, an exposure
described as six minutes and four hubs when it was seven minutes and six-then-
four, an "attribution unresolved" for nine rows that were fully attributable, and
a concurrent-sweep claim contradicted by the table directly above it.

**What made it converge is that each session published the thing that cost it
something.**

- The credit-audit session falsified four of its own assertions, disclosed the
  defect that consumed two of this run's cold renders before anyone else could
  have found it, and — asked a direct question — published the two weaknesses in
  its own best evidence when asked a direct question it could have deflected: no
  body size, no negative control.
- `-59` declined a vindication it could have kept, on the grounds that the same
  push that made the sweep cold would have destroyed it had the timing differed.
  It also refused a finding credited to it that it had never observed, because a
  real observation attributed to the wrong session sends a future reader to
  someone who never saw it.
- The team lead reversed two of its own instructions — an expectation of
  uncredited covers, and an "attribution unresolved" — and named its own
  reading of the file as the same defect this log is about — and then, asked
  nothing, went back and named that error more precisely than the generous
  version it had been offered. See below; it is the sharpest instance of the
  record-bytes finding in the whole document.
- This run disclosed an instrument gap that cost it the one measurement it most
  needed, and named its own proof method as the cause of a production incident.

**The record-bytes finding, demonstrated live by the person insisting on it.**
Three times the team lead reported a clause missing that was already in the file.
The generous reading — mine, in an earlier draft — was that the file kept moving
between the read and the message. **That was wrong, and the lead corrected it
against its own interest.**

The extraction was:

```
sed -n '/cold by accident, which has to travel/,/^$/p'
```

That range terminates at the first blank line, so it printed the accident
paragraph and stopped **exactly where the appended paragraph began**. The end of
the extraction was then treated as the end of the section, and absence concluded.

And the same command block carried the contradicting evidence: a `grep -c` for
`no body size|never looked` returned **5**. Five matches for text about to be
declared missing. The `sed` output was read, the conclusion formed, and the
dissenting count sitting directly above it never reconciled.

**That is the record-bytes finding exactly**: an instrument that could not see
the thing it was checking for, and an operator who did not read the field that
disagreed. Committed while verifying that this log described that same finding
correctly, by the person who had spent three rounds pressing for it to be filed.

It is the strongest argument in the document for the rule, and it is stronger
than anything this run could have written about itself — **a lesson demonstrated
by the person insisting on it costs its author something, and that is what makes
it evidence rather than advice.**

**None of those disclosures was extracted by review.** Every one arrived because
the session holding it decided the record mattered more than its own account of
the day. That is the whole mechanism, and it is worth naming because it is not
guaranteed by any process rule written above — a run that reports only what
survives scrutiny is indistinguishable, at the time, from one that had nothing to
report.

### Two things that went right and should be kept

**Escalating the `metaDescription` rather than trimming it quietly.** The owner
approved the exact edit proposed, so the outcome was identical to acting alone —
but it is now on the record with both strings, both counts, and an authority
behind it, which is what a future reader needs when they find that article text
was altered. The two unaffected articles shipped while the question was open, so
it blocked nothing. **Raise the question, keep working on the part that does not
depend on the answer.** That should be the default whenever a hard rule in a
brief collides with a hard constraint in the code.

**Reporting the contaminated measurements as contaminated.** Two of the three
first requests were spent before this run could take them, and every cover-credit
observation here is serial or warm — the exact conditions under which a
load-dependent race hides. Presenting either as clean would have produced a
confident, wrong finding and undercut a real defect a peer session had correctly
diagnosed. **A proof is only worth what its weakest attribution is worth**, and
the weak ones have to be labelled at the time, because nobody can tell afterwards
which fetch was cold.
